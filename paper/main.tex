\title{From System Ranking to Decision Value:\\ How Much Human Evaluation Do Automatic Evaluators Actually Save?}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Automatic evaluators, from learned metrics to LLM judges, are increasingly credited with the human labels they save, and
that credit is usually inferred from their correlation with human scores. We measure it directly for a concrete decision:
certifying, from a human audit, that a selected system is within $\eps$ of the best of a menu of close competitors. An
evaluator's human-evidence saving (HES) is the fraction of human labels, pilot included, that it saves for this decision
over the best fixed annotation design that uses no evaluator. Across machine translation (WMT22 MQM; 31 segment-level
metric variants and open LLM judges), chat (LMArena) and retrieval, with pre-registered MT and chat tests,
evaluator quality turns out to depend on the level at which it is measured: metrics with system-level correlations of
0.93--0.99 reach correlations of only 0.11--0.38 on the paired differences a close decision depends on. Correspondingly,
over 33 informative cells from 19 two-system MT decisions (300 simulated audits, 34 evaluators), the mean evaluator
saves a median 0--2\% of human labels on top of a judge-free design, which itself saves a median 5.5\% (8--16\% in the
four pre-registered cells, mostly by not re-rating identical outputs; 41--56\% in retrieval). With 2,000 audits per
cell (31 metric variants) the picture sharpens rather than changes: the design saves 4.6\%, the mean evaluator 2.3\%,
the ten strongest 3.9\% and the post-hoc best 6.0\% with a refitted coefficient, and a 10\% saving is ruled out for 94\%
of decision--evaluator pairs.
Realised savings follow $\rho^2(1-P/J)$, so they are a property of the regime as much as of the evaluator: with
decision-level $\rho\le0.43$ and a pilot that takes up to two thirds of the cost, this ceiling is below 5\% in 94\% of
cases, and even a negligible pilot would leave it at $\rho^2\le0.19$. Freezing the correction coefficient on a 50-item pilot
costs a further 2.5--4.3 points, which refitting on all labels removes in our simulations, with average coverage near nominal.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Human judgments remain the reference for comparing NLP systems, and they are expensive. Automatic evaluators promise to
stretch them: score everything automatically and spend human effort only on correcting the evaluator's bias, as in
prediction-powered inference \citep[PPI;][]{angelopoulos2023ppi,angelopoulos2023ppipp}. How much an evaluator saves
under such a correction is now measured directly \citep{boyeau2024autoeval,chen2026noisy}, and \citet{gao2026ppsr}
propose to rank MT metrics by the annotation they save (PPSR), which is governed by the squared correlation between
human and metric score \emph{differences}.

PPSR is already a realised quantity: \citet{gao2026ppsr} verify by resampling that, under uniform labelling with the
coefficient fitted on the evaluation labels, the strongest WMT22 metrics save about 9--15\% of the labels a paired
comparison needs. We do not revisit that number. We ask how much of it is left for the decision an evaluation is usually
run for, and how much of it a competent annotation design obtains without any evaluator. A team has a short list of
strong systems that are close in quality, and wants to certify from a human audit that the one it picks is within $\eps$
of the best. Three things separate this setting from the asymptotic picture. First, the decision is local: the systems
that matter are the top few, not the whole field over which system-level correlation is computed. Second, a human audit
need not sample uniformly: items whose paired difference is known to be zero (identical outputs, documents outside every
system's cutoff) need no label, and others can be sampled in proportion to how much they can move the decision; these labels are saved
without any evaluator, which should be credited only with what it adds on top. Third, the
coefficient that corrects the evaluator must itself be estimated from the few human labels the audit has, or be fitted on
all of them with a variance estimate that ignores the coefficient's error.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A decision-level measure of evaluator value that credits the design first.} The human-evidence saving
$\HES=1-\Jt(\text{design}+\text{evaluator})/\Jt(\text{best fixed judge-free design})$, with $\Jt$ the number of unique
human labels, pilot included, at which the decision is certified with probability $1/2$ (\S\ref{sec:setup}).
\item \textbf{The design is credited first and usually saves more; the evaluator's increment is small on average
and depends on the evaluator.} Over 33 informative cells from 19 two-system WMT22 decisions (300 audits, 34
evaluators), a judge-free design saves a median 5.5\% of uniform-sampling labels; the mean evaluator adds 0--2\% on
the same design (0.6--2.8\%
for the ten with the highest system-level correlation) and the design saves more in 76--94\% of cells under every
coefficient rule. In the four pre-registered cells the best of 31 metric variants adds at most 7\% to a design saving of
8--16\% with the pilot-fixed coefficient (refitted: up to 15\%). Retrieval: 41--56\% vs.\ 1--9 further points (\S\ref{sec:design}).
\item \textbf{What the decisions rule out.} With 2,000 simulated audits per cell (31 metric variants) the mean
evaluator saves 2.3\%, the ten strongest 3.9\% and the post-hoc best 6.0\% (refitted), and a one-sided 95\% bound on a
single evaluator's saving over the design is below 5\% in 64\% and below 10\% in 94\% of decision--evaluator pairs (29\%
and 88\% for the ten strongest): the savings are real, small on average, and bounded (\S\ref{sec:design}).
\item \textbf{Evaluator quality collapses across levels.} Metric variants with system-level
Pearson 0.93--0.99 reach decision-level correlations of only 0.11--0.38; 10 of the 31 rank the top-4 zh$\to$en systems
in exactly reversed order (\S\ref{sec:levels}).
\item \textbf{Savings are capped by decision-level $\rho$ and the pilot.} With the coefficient refitted on all labels,
realised HES follows the tax-free ceiling $\rho^2(1-P/J)$, which is below 5\% in 94\% of
decision--evaluator pairs. Freezing the coefficient on a 50-item pilot costs a further 4.3 [3.5, 5.2] (en$\to$de) and
2.5 [2.0, 3.1] (zh$\to$en) points over the 31 variants; refitting removes this tax with nominal coverage in our
simulations, and cross-fitting only partly. Smaller pilots (25, 10 segments) do not change the picture: the mean
evaluator then adds 0.3--0.7\% refitted and loses 3--8\% with a frozen coefficient (\S\ref{sec:law}).
\item \textbf{Pre-registered tests, with failures reported.} Three locks fixed hypotheses before the corresponding
evaluator outputs were joined with human labels (limits in \S\ref{sec:data}); of ten hypotheses, H2, H5 and H6 failed
and H4 failed in chat (\S\ref{sec:prereg}).
\end{enumerate}
We propose no estimator: the corrected estimator is PPI++ with a pilot-fixed coefficient, and the allocation is
active statistical inference \citep{zrnic2024active} for the decision's functional.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Decision.} A menu $\mathcal M$ of systems is evaluated on a fixed set of $N$ items with human utilities
$u_m(i)$ and means $\mu_m$. An audit labels a pilot of $P$ items fully, picks the candidate $\hat m$ with the best pilot
mean, and \emph{certifies} it if for every competitor $j$ a one-sided upper bound on $\mu_j-\mu_{\hat m}$ is at most
$\eps$ (normal bound, Bonferroni over the $|\mathcal M|-1$ competitors, $\alpha=0.10$). A certificate is wrong if
$\max_j\mu_j-\mu_{\hat m}>\eps$. Menus are deliberately hard, built from the full human labels (the top-4 systems by MQM; the closest Arena pairs);
their construction is not part of the audit.

\paragraph{Estimator.} After the pilot, items are drawn by Poisson sampling with probabilities $\pi_i$. For
$D_j=u_j-u_{\hat m}$ and an evaluator's predicted difference $\hat D_j$,
\begin{equation}
\hat\Delta_j=\tfrac1N\Big[\sum_{\text{pilot}}D_j+\sum_{\text{rest}}\lambda_j\hat D_j+\sum_{i\in S}\tfrac{D_j(i)-\lambda_j\hat D_j(i)}{\pi_i}\Big],\label{eq:est}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_j=\max(0,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(D_j,\hat D_j)/\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat D_j))$ fitted on the pilot and
frozen; $\lambda=0$ is the human-only estimator; where two outputs coincide we set $\hat D_j(i)=0$ with $D_j(i)=0$. Given the pilot, (\ref{eq:est}) is then unbiased for the finite-population
mean for any evaluator (items the design never samples contribute only $\lambda\hat D_j$, which the zero rule keeps exact; 13--14 of the 31 WMT22 metrics score identical strings slightly differently, by at most $7\times10^{-6}$ in the mean difference); its variance is estimated by $\sum_S(1-\pi_i)R_i^2/(\pi_i^2N^2)$, $R=D-\lambda\hat D$.

\paragraph{Designs.} \emph{Uniform}: constant $\pi$; every menu output of a sampled item is labelled, identical
strings included, as in WMT22's own MQM campaign, which rated each system's output separately (42\% of en$\to$de and
18\% of zh$\to$en rated outputs share their string with another system's; 26\% of such groups received different
scores). We treat those differences as rater noise around one quality per string: the estimand averages the ratings of
identical strings, whose paired difference is then zero; sharing one random rating instead changes little (App.~\ref{app:sens}). \emph{Dedup}: uniform over items where some
competitor's output differs from the candidate's, identical strings rated once, and $D_j=0$ used exactly where outputs
coincide. \emph{Weighted}: as dedup, with items sampled in proportion to a label-free proxy of $|D|$ (in retrieval the
closed-form decision weight of each document; in MT $\sqrt{\sum_j\hat\sigma^2_j(b)}$, with $\hat\sigma^2_j(b)$ the pilot
mean of $D_j^2$ in one of three bins $b$ of the chrF-based dissimilarity of the two outputs: below its median, its third
and its fourth quartile; identical outputs form a fourth bin of weight zero. A four-quartile variant changes nothing of
substance (App.~\ref{app:sens}). The \emph{best fixed judge-free baseline} of a cell is the cheapest
of these human-only designs there; it is a benchmark, not a procedure an auditor selects. An evaluator enters as the
control variate of (\ref{eq:est}) on top of a design.

\paragraph{Cost.} $\Jt$ counts unique human labels, pilot included, at which a pre-committed budget certifies in half of
300 simulated audits (linear interpolation over a geometric grid of 14 budgets up to the whole post-pilot set). The 50-segment MT pilot costs 162
(en$\to$de) and 182 (zh$\to$en) labels, 42--68\% of $\Jt$ on en$\to$de; an evaluator can act only on the rest (the
factor $1-P/J$ of \S\ref{sec:law}). Intervals are paired bootstraps over the 300 simulated audits of a
fixed test set: they reflect Monte-Carlo, not data-sampling, uncertainty.

\section{Data, evaluators and protocol}\label{sec:data}
\textbf{MT:} WMT22 MQM \citep{freitag2021mqm,freitag2022wmt22metrics} en$\to$de (1{,}315 segments) and zh$\to$en (1{,}875), Google weights,
$u=-\min(\mathrm{MQM},25)/25$, identical output strings of a segment share the mean of their ratings; menus of the four best WMT submissions
(MBR systems excluded). Evaluators: the 31 WMT22 metric variants available at segment level in mt-metrics-eval \citep{freitag2022wmt22metrics}
(reference-based variants scored against refA, including COMET-22 \citep{rei2022comet}, and reference-free QE variants),
plus our own COMET-22 run and Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3} and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral} with the GEMBA-DA prompt \citep{kocmi2023gemba}.
\textbf{Chat:} LMArena human-preference battles \citep{chiang2024arena}; the six English single-turn pairs with the
smallest win-rate gaps among those with $\ge300$ battles (311--472 battles; gaps 0.011--0.084). Evaluators: Qwen3-8B and
Mistral-7B as pairwise judges (both presentation orders averaged), ``the longer response wins''.
\textbf{Retrieval:} a re-analysis of fixed-budget audits from an earlier study \citep{anonymous2026retrieval}: three cutoff policies over a
shared ranked pool (an expected-F1 gate, a score threshold, a learned truncation) compared by precision at their cutoffs,
$\eps$ in precision units, on four fully judged collections
(ANTIQUE, CAsT 2019, DBpedia-Entity, TREC DL 21--23; \citealp{hashemi2020antique,dalton2020cast,hasibi2017dbpedia,craswell2021dl21,craswell2022dl22,craswell2023dl23}) with Qwen3-8B (UMBRELA prompt; \citealp{upadhyay2024umbrela}) and Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B \citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding} as judges; pilot
20 queries. MT and chat use a 50-item pilot (ablations: 100 for MT, 25 for chat), $\eps\in\{0.01,0.02\}$ (MT) and
$\{0.05,0.1\}$ (chat).

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Lock v0.8 (MT, chat) was committed before any LLM or COMET judgment was joined with a human
label; v0.9 replaced the uninformative v0.8 chat decision (gaps 0.10--0.56, settled by the pilot alone) by close
pairs before they were judged; v1.1 fixed the 31-metric test before any of its savings were computed. All locks were
committed within about four hours on public data whose human labels exist; before v0.8 the human-only MT designs had
been run in development (they saved 8--19\% over uniform sampling and chrF about nothing, so H1 was not a blind test),
and v1.1 followed the exploratory estimation-tax analyses of the open judges, so H9 tests on 31 new evaluators a
phenomenon first seen on four. All locks, deviations and outcomes are in the supplementary material.

\section{Evaluator quality depends on the level of measurement}\label{sec:levels}
Figure~\ref{fig:levels} places the 30 positively correlated WMT22 metric variants (REUSE omitted) on three levels against their realised HES. At the global level
(Pearson over the 11 WMT submissions) nearly all strong metrics are above 0.9. Among the four closest systems the ranking is fragile: MetricX-XL-DA and MetricX-XXL-DA reach system-level
Pearson 0.994--0.995 on zh$\to$en and Kendall $\tau=0$ among the top four. The second- and third-ranked systems differ by
only 0.0009 (en$\to$de) and 0.0017 (zh$\to$en) in utility (standard error 0.002--0.004), so $\tau=0$ can reflect fragility;
but 10 of the 31 zh$\to$en variants (system-level Pearson 0.83--0.96; BLEU, spBLEU, chrF, BERTScore, YiSi, SEScore, MEE)
reach $\tau=-1$, which also reverses the first-ranked system's lead over each of the other three (2.3--3.5 standard errors). The
quantity that governs a control variate is the correlation $\rho$ of per-item paired differences, and it is low
everywhere: 0.30--0.38 for the best metrics on en$\to$de (mean over the menu's pairs), 0.21--0.25 on zh$\to$en, and 0.17--0.29 for COMET-22 and the
Qwen3-8B GEMBA judge (system-level 0.97--0.99; here medians over pairs of the top-6 systems), while the Mistral-7B GEMBA
judge reaches only 0.04--0.09. Part of this is label noise: in the en$\to$de three-rater re-annotation, two raters'
paired differences on the same items correlate only 0.31--0.41, so even a perfect evaluator would reach $\rho\approx0.59$
against the single ratings an audit collects; the best metrics attain one half to two thirds of that (App.~\ref{app:sens}). Across metrics, HES is ranked slightly better by $\rho$ than by system-level Pearson (Spearman 0.39 vs.\
0.36 on en$\to$de, 0.22 vs.\ 0.07 on zh$\to$en; H11), but neither predicts it well: all real metrics sit where the
saving is small (\S\ref{sec:law}).

\section{Where the savings come from}\label{sec:design}
Figure~\ref{fig:waterfall} and Table~\ref{tab:main} decompose the saving over uniform sampling into the part a judge-free design obtains and the
part the best evaluator adds on top. In retrieval, skipping documents that no comparison depends on and sampling the rest
by decision weight saves 41--56\% of the labels of uniform sampling; the Qwen3-8B judge adds 1--9 points of that total
(HES 3--20\% over the design). In MT, identical outputs (per pair of menu systems, 14--24\% of en$\to$de and 5--16\% of zh$\to$en segments) need no label,
and many paired MQM differences are exactly zero (53--59\% and 40--48\%); exploiting this saves 16\% (en$\to$de) and 8\%
(zh$\to$en) at $\eps=0.01$. The best of the 31 metric variants, chosen \emph{post hoc} and therefore favoured, adds 7\%
on the same design in both language pairs, and the median metric adds nothing ($-1\%$ to $0\%$). In all four pre-registered MT cells the design's saving (8--16\%)
exceeds the best metric's increment (1--7\%; H8, 4/4; H1 with the open judges, 4/4). Most of this is deduplication: not re-rating identical outputs saves 13\% and 6\% on en$\to$de and all 8\% on zh$\to$en.

\paragraph{Across 30 decisions.} Four cells are few, and their Monte-Carlo intervals are wide. We therefore repeated
the comparison (exploratory, post-lock) on every two-system decision among the top six WMT22 systems of each language
pair: 30 decisions, two $\eps$ each, all 31 metric variants, COMET-22 and both GEMBA judges (Figure~\ref{fig:decisions}).
In 33 of the 60 cells, from 19 decisions (4 en$\to$de, 29 zh$\to$en), at least 30\% of the uniform-sampling cost falls
after the pilot (App.~\ref{app:sens}); in the others the pilot nearly settles the decision. Over these 33 cells the judge-free design saves a
median 5.5\% (interquartile range 3--10\%), and the mean evaluator a median $-0.1\%$ with the pilot coefficient, 1.6\%
refitted and 0.4\% cross-fitted; the design saves more than the mean evaluator in 94\%, 82\% and 91\% of the cells (91\%, 76\% and 79\% for the ten evaluators with the highest system-level correlation). The best evaluator
chosen post hoc beats the design in about half of them; at 300 audits its lead over the mean evaluator (median 4.3--5.0
points) matches what picking the largest of 34 equal evaluators produces under the Monte-Carlo noise (4.3--4.8 points;
significant at 0.05 in at most 6\% of cells), so that test is uninformative and is repeated below with 2,000 audits. Only 5.5\% of MT decision--evaluator pairs have a refitted
HES interval above zero.

\paragraph{What the decisions rule out.} With 300 audits the median 95\% interval of one evaluator's HES is $\pm6.6$
points, too wide to call a saving small. We therefore re-ran the 30 decisions
with 2,000 audits per cell (the 31 metric variants, otherwise identical), which narrows the interval to $\pm2.3$ points; 36 informative cells. The medians hold: the design saves 4.6\%,
the mean evaluator 0.2 / 2.3 / 0.9\% (pilot / refit / cross-fit), and the design saves more in 100 / 89 / 100\% of
cells (89 / 61 / 78\% against the mean of the ten strongest evaluators, which reaches 3.9\% refitted). The post-hoc
best (median 6.0\% refitted) now leads the mean by more than selection noise explains (3.3 vs.\ 1.8 points; $p<0.05$ in
72\% of cells refitted, 33\% pilot, 28\% cross-fit), so evaluators do differ; the design beats it in 39\% of cells
refitted and 61\% under the other rules. With the refitted coefficient the one-sided 95\% upper bound of a single evaluator's HES over the design is below
5\% in 64\% and below 10\% in 94\% of the 1,116 decision--evaluator pairs (29\% and 88\% for the ten evaluators with the
highest system-level correlation; 89\% and 99\% with the pilot coefficient). For the \emph{mean} evaluator of a cell the
bound is below 5\% in 89\% and below 10\% in 97\% of cells; the exception is one en$\to$de decision at $\eps=0.01$,
where the mean evaluator saves 7\% (bound 13\%) and the design 14\%. Many small refitted savings are now distinguishable from zero (53\% of pairs; median 2.0\%, interquartile range
0.8--3.6\%): real, and small. In labels: of a median decision's 256, the design saves 17, the mean evaluator 6 (refitted), the post-hoc best 17.

\section{From correlation potential to realised saving}\label{sec:law}
\begin{proposition}[Finite-pilot cost approximation]\label{prop:law}
Let a design need $L_0$ post-pilot labels for a fixed decision without an evaluator, and let the evaluator's paired
differences $\hat D$ have correlation $\rho$ with the human ones $D$. Assume that, over informative items (those with a
non-zero difference), $D$ is a linear projection on $\hat D$ with a residual whose variance does not depend on $\hat D$
(homoskedastic; joint normality is a sufficient case), and that $\lambda$ is fitted by least squares on
a pilot independent of the post-pilot sample, with effective informative size $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (the number of pilot
items that contribute variation to the paired regression, approximated by those with a non-zero paired difference; $\lambda_j$ and $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ are per competitor $j$). Then, to first order in
$1/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and under the normal fixed-budget approximation, the variance of the corrected estimator is
$(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ times that of the uncorrected one and
\begin{equation}
\HES\approx\Big[\rho^2-\frac{1-\rho^2}{P_{\mathrm{eff}}}\Big]\Big(1-\frac{P}{J_0}\Big),\qquad J_0=P+L_0 .\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Without homoskedasticity the variance of $\hat\lambda$ takes a sandwich form and the tax term changes accordingly; the
semi-synthetic evaluators below satisfy the assumption, the real ones need not. The proposition is bookkeeping of known results, an interpretive approximation rather than a theorem; $\Jt$ is a
median-power budget, which under the normal approximation scales with the expected variance. The first factor combines the PPI++ variance reduction $\rho^2$ \citep{angelopoulos2023ppipp} with the classical
inflation of a regression estimator whose coefficient is estimated \citep{cochran1977}; the second is the share of the
cost that remains after the pilot. The derivation is in Appendix~\ref{app:law}. Equation~(\ref{eq:law}) implies that an
evaluator with $\rho^2<1/(P_{\mathrm{eff}}+1)$ costs labels, about $\rho<0.2$ for the 50-item MT pilot, where half the
paired differences are zero.

\paragraph{Controlled variation.} To test (\ref{eq:law}) over the whole range we built semi-synthetic evaluators, human
utility plus Gaussian noise scaled to a target $\rho\in\{0.1,\dots,0.9\}$, and ran them through the same audits (MT
pilots 50 and 100, three Arena pairs; 126 cells). Predicted and realised HES correlate 0.97 (mean absolute error 0.04;
Figure~\ref{fig:dial} in App.~\ref{app:dial}); the tax-free ceiling does as well (0.97, 0.04) and the law over-predicts by 0.02, so
the experiment supports the $\rho^2$ and dilution factors more than the tax term. The realised saving is 10\% at
$\rho=0.5$ and 31\% at $\rho=0.8$ (identical outputs share one synthetic score, so known-zero differences stay zero; App.~\ref{app:dial}). For the open evaluators on the two-system menus ($\rho\le0.36$) predicted and
realised HES correlate only 0.20: realised savings (interquartile range $-1\%$ to $+1\%$) are dominated by Monte-Carlo
noise and exceed the ceiling by more than 0.03 in only 2--4\% of cells.

\paragraph{Held-out year: what should an auditor do?} The comparisons above choose the cheapest judge-free design and the best
evaluator after seeing the costs. We therefore fixed four strategies on WMT22 and applied them, unchanged, to the WMT23 MQM
sets (en$\to$de 460 paragraph-level segments, 11 systems; zh$\to$en 1{,}177 segments, 14 systems; the 42 submitted metric
variants; the same 15 two-system menus per language pair, pilot 50, $\eps\in\{0.01,0.02\}$; pre-registered as lock
v1.2, App.~\ref{app:heldout}): S1 the dedup design alone; S2 dedup plus COMET-22, the strongest WMT22 metric submitted under
the same name in both years; S3 dedup plus the evaluator with the highest pilot $\rho$; S4 as S3 unless that $\rho$ is below
the cost law's break-even of 0.2. With refitting, S2 and S3 cost 3.1\% and 3.3\% less than S1 on WMT23 (cheaper in 92\% and
85\% of the 26 informative cells; WMT22: 4.4\% and 3.2\%, 85\% and 88\%), the post-hoc best 7.9\% less (within selection noise
in every cell), and selection costs more than 2\% extra in 4\% of cells. With the frozen coefficient, S2 saves 0.3\% and pilot
selection costs 3.4\% more than S1 (cheaper in 19\% of cells; WMT22: 27\%). Abstention changes nothing (2--4\% of audits). The
new judges raise decision-level $\rho$ to 0.36 (MetricX-23, XCOMET) and the best refitted increment to 5--6\%, still below
the design's own saving in most cells. The rule that survives both years is therefore: deduplicate, add one strong
evaluator, refit its coefficient, and do not spend the pilot on choosing the evaluator or on freezing the coefficient.

\paragraph{The estimation tax on real metrics.} Replacing the pilot-fitted coefficient by the population-optimal one
isolates the tax. Over the 31 metric variants it is 4.3 [3.5, 5.2] points on en$\to$de and 2.5 [2.0, 3.1] on zh$\to$en
(metric-level bootstrap; H9). For the five metrics with the largest $\rho^2$ (0.11--0.15 on en$\to$de, as $(\bar\rho)^2$
or as PPSR's mean $\rho^2$, within the 0.09--0.15 PPSR reports), the realised saving over uniform sampling is between
$-4\%$ and $+6\%$ (en$\to$de) and $-6\%$ and $+1\%$ (zh$\to$en), below the correlation-based saving in every cell (H10,
under both definitions). With the oracle coefficient the same en$\to$de metrics reach 6--12\% on the decision-aware
design: the asymptotic saving is not wrong, it is what an auditor who already knew $\lambda$ would obtain.

\paragraph{Is the tax an artefact of freezing $\lambda$?} Mostly. Standard PPI++ fits $\lambda$ on all labelled
items. We re-ran the eight strongest metric variants per language pair, COMET-22 and the two GEMBA judges with the
coefficient refitted on the pilot and every post-pilot label, and cross-fitted over two folds (App.~\ref{app:robust}). Mean HES on the weighted design rises from $+0.4\%$ (pilot coefficient) to
$+6.8\%$ with refitting and $+1.6\%$ with cross-fitting on en$\to$de, against $+6.7\%$ for the oracle coefficient; on
zh$\to$en from $-0.4\%$ to $+2.0\%$ and $+1.1\%$, against $+1.3\%$; on the six chat pairs from $+1.1\%$ to $+2.3\%$ and $+1.5\%$, against $+2.1\%$. Its variance estimate ignores the coefficient's estimation
error, but over the 30 decisions and the six chat pairs the refitted upper bound covered the true difference at 0.90
(nominal 0.90), also at the budget nearest each design's $\Jt$, where it certifies (refit 0.902, cross-fit 0.903,
pilot 0.903, human-only 0.904), and under boundary stress its largest type-I error was 0.05. Cross-fitting recovers
less because each fold's coefficient rests on half of the labels; a practitioner who needs no budget plan can refit,
one who wants the variance estimate to account for the coefficient should cross-fit or freeze (the bound itself is
calibrated by simulation, not proved). Refitting can exceed the oracle, which is the population
least-squares coefficient, optimal for uniform sampling only; on the weighted design the coefficient that minimises the
realised Poisson-design variance differs from it by a median 0.0\% and at most 3.7\% of that variance (App.~\ref{app:law}), so the two are interchangeable here. Across the 30 decisions (Figure~\ref{fig:decisions}b) refitted HES
follows the tax-free ceiling $\rho^2(1-P/J)$ (median deviation $-0.4$ points, correlation 0.42 over 1{,}158
decision--evaluator points), whereas with the pilot coefficient it falls a median 2.3 points below it; 94\% of the
ceilings are below 5\%, because decision-level $\rho$ is at most 0.43 and the pilot is a median 39\% of the cost (range 15--66\%). The tax is therefore the price of committing to the coefficient before the audit (to plan the budget, say), not a
property of the evaluator; refitting remains exploratory (the same labels set the coefficient and the inference). Even refitted, in the four locked cells the mean of the eleven (6.9, 6.7, 4.8, $-0.3\%$) stays below the design
(15.9, 9.0, 8.4, 8.5\%).

\paragraph{Pilot size trades tax for fixed cost.} Equation~(\ref{eq:law}) predicts that a larger pilot lowers the tax
$(1-\rho^2)/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ but raises the fixed cost $P$. Both effects appear. Doubling the MT pilot to 100 segments
shrinks the losses of the weak LLM judges (Mistral-7B, en$\to$de, $\eps=0.01$: $-7.1\%\to-0.9\%$) and halves the saving of a semi-synthetic $\rho=0.9$ evaluator (32\%$\to$15\%) because the
pilot becomes most of the cost. A smaller pilot does not rescue the evaluators either: with pilots of 25 and 10 segments
on the 30 two-system decisions (49 and 60 informative cells, now including en$\to$de; pilot share 27\% and 13\% of the
cost; the 31 metric variants), the design saves a median 4.3\% and 4.4\%, the mean evaluator adds 0.7\% and 0.3\%
refitted ($-2.7\%$ and $-7.7\%$ with the pilot coefficient, whose tax grows as $1/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$), and the design
saves more in 86\% and 90\% of cells (App.~\ref{app:sens}).

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Judges and metrics as system rankers.} JuStRank \citep{gera2025justrank} and \citet{gao2025reeval} show that
instance-level accuracy does not determine system-ranking quality and that ranking degrades for close systems;
\citet{zhang2026utility} separate judgment quality from downstream utility. In MT, system-level correlation is driven by outliers
\citep{mathur2020tangled}, close systems are better compared by pairwise accuracy \citep{kocmi2021ship,deutsch2023ties},
correlations on close pairs are much lower \citep{deutsch2022reexamining}, and such comparisons are often underpowered
\citep{card2020little}. We follow quality one level further, to the labels a close decision requires.
\paragraph{Prediction-powered evaluation.} Control variates over human and metric scores, with modest savings \citep{chaganty2018price}; PPI and PPI++ \citep{angelopoulos2023ppi,angelopoulos2023ppipp,gligoric2024unconfident}, active
inference \citep{zrnic2024active}, AutoEval and prediction-powered ranking on Chatbot Arena
\citep{boyeau2024autoeval,chatzi2024ppr}, noisy-judge inference \citep{chen2026noisy}, PRECISE \citep{divekar2026precise},
and the bound of \citet{dorner2025limits} that an evaluator no better than the evaluated model saves at most half the labels. Closest to us, \citet{gao2026ppsr} rank WMT metrics by the annotation they
save under prediction-powered evaluation (PPSR, the mean squared correlation of paired differences) and verify the
saving by resampling, with uniform labelling over all systems and the coefficient estimated on the evaluation labels.
PPSR answers which evaluator saves most; we ask what the saving is worth once the decision is local, the design is
credited first and the coefficient is fitted on a pilot. The two are complementary: PPSR's $\rho^2$ is the first factor
of our cost law.
\paragraph{Annotation design.} Choosing which items humans label is studied for model comparison
\citep{sawade2012active}, active testing \citep{kossen2021active}, IR test collections
\citep{sakai2016topic,li2017active}, and within PPI by cross-prediction \citep{zrnic2024cross} and stratification
\citep{fisch2024stratified}. Our judge-free designs are simple instances, credited before the evaluator.

\section{Conclusion: three numbers to check before an audit}
For a close system decision, an evaluator's value is set by the correlation of the differences the decision depends on,
diluted by the pilot's fixed cost and by what a judge-free design already obtains; WMT22 metrics and open LLM judges then
save a few percent of human labels on average, the strongest somewhat more, and the typical one less than organising
the annotation itself. The results reduce
to a rule a team can apply before paying for an evaluator: (i) its decision-level $\rho$ on the systems at stake, from
a prior campaign or the pilot, not its system-level agreement; (ii) the pilot's share of the cost, $P/J$, which a
human-only pilot prediction supplies; (iii) what the judge-free design already saves, known in advance. On a held-out year the strategies these numbers imply (dedup, one strong evaluator, a refitted coefficient) saved 3\% over the judge-free design, and pilot-based evaluator selection added nothing a fixed strong evaluator did not already give. Under the assumptions of (\ref{eq:law}) the increment
is about $\rho^2(1-P/J)$, less $(1-\rho^2)/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ of the post-pilot share if the coefficient is frozen: for
the WMT22 metrics ($\rho\le0.43$, $P/J\approx0.4$) at most 11\%, typically 2--5\%, against design savings of 5--16\%.

\section*{Limitations}
The menus are built from full human labels to be deliberately hard; easier decisions leave less to save for any method.
The certificate uses a normal bound whose calibration we checked by simulation, not proved. MT savings are computed on
the MQM-rated subsets of WMT22 (two language pairs), chat on six pairs from a 2023 Arena release, retrieval on
re-analysed audits of an earlier study with two judges. The cost law is a first-order approximation; it describes
controlled variation of $\rho$ and bounds, but does not predict cell by cell, the savings of real evaluators. The LLM judges are open 7--8B models; frontier
LLM judges are not evaluated here, and their decision-level $\rho$ may be higher. The evaluator enters only as a control
variate on the audit; its other uses (choosing the candidate, building the menu, stratifying the sample) are outside
the decision value we measure. HES depends on the pilot size, and larger pilots
lower the estimation tax while raising the fixed cost (\S\ref{sec:law}). The results should not be read as showing
that automatic evaluators are useless: they concern close decisions, small human pilots and evaluators with
decision-level $\rho\le0.43$; by the cost law, an evaluator with $\rho\approx0.8$ would save about a third of the labels with our pilots, and more
with a smaller pilot share, but against single WMT22 ratings label noise caps $\rho$ near 0.6 (App.~\ref{app:sens}), so such
savings also require more reliable human labels. Uncertainty is Monte-Carlo over simulated audits of one fixed test set per language pair;
items and raters are not resampled. We report $\Jt$; at 80\% power savings differ and are often censored by the budget
grid (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The estimand treats identical strings as one quality; sharing one random rating per string
instead leaves the design savings essentially unchanged (App.~\ref{app:sens}), but under an as-rated estimand, in which identical
strings may differ in quality, their rating noise would be variance that deduplication does not remove. The retrieval evidence rests on a companion study
under review.

\section{Derivation of the cost approximation}\label{app:law}
Write $D$ for the paired difference of an informative item, $\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(D)$, and $\hat D$ for the evaluator's
prediction with $\mathrm{Corr}(D,\hat D)=\rho$. For a fixed coefficient $\lambda$ the residual $R=D-\lambda\hat D$ has
variance $\sigma^2-2\lambda\rho\sigma\hat\sigma+\lambda^2\hat\sigma^2$, minimised at $\lambda^\star=\rho\sigma/\hat\sigma$ to
$\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)$ \citep{angelopoulos2023ppipp}. With $\hat\lambda$ the least-squares coefficient from a
pilot of effective informative size $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$, independent of the post-pilot sample, and a homoskedastic residual ($\mathrm{E}[R^2\mid\hat D]=\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)$), $\mathrm{E}[(\hat\lambda-\lambda^\star)^2]\approx
\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)/(P_{\mathrm{eff}}\hat\sigma^2)$, so the expected residual variance is
$\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ \citep[\S7]{cochran1977}. Under the normal fixed-budget approximation the
post-pilot labels needed to bring $z\sqrt{v/L}$ below the slack scale with the variance factor, so
$L\approx L_0(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ and $1-L/L_0\approx\rho^2-(1-\rho^2)/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ to first order in
$1/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$. With the pilot cost $P$ common to both arms, $\HES=1-(P+L)/(P+L_0)=(1-L/L_0)(1-P/J_0)$. Clipping
$\lambda$ at zero bounds the loss of an evaluator with $\rho\le0$ by the variance of $\hat\lambda$ only; zero-difference
items reduce $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ below $P$, which is why MT, with about half its paired differences zero, pays more tax.
In general, $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\lambda)\approx\mathrm{E}[(\hat D-\mathrm{E}\hat D)^2R^2]/(P_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat D)^2)$ (sandwich form), which reduces to the expression above under homoskedasticity; heavy-tailed or heteroskedastic residuals, as with sparse MQM differences, raise the tax.
The coefficient that minimises the variance of (\ref{eq:est}) under a given design, $\sum_i(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i\,(D_i-\lambda\hat D_i)^2$, is a
least-squares fit with weights $(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i$ and equals the population coefficient only under uniform sampling; on the
weighted design of the top-4 menus (31 metrics, three semi-synthetic evaluators, 20 pilots) the population coefficient's variance
exceeds it by a median 0.0\%, a 90th percentile of 0.2\% and at most 3.7\%, so $\rho^2$ is used as an interpretive approximation, not a bound.
