\title{From System Ranking to Decision Value:\\ How Much Human Evaluation Do Automatic Evaluators Actually Save?}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Automatic evaluators are credited with the human labels they save, and the credit is usually inferred from their
correlation with human scores. We measure it for the decision an evaluation is usually run for: certifying, from a human
audit, that a chosen system is within $\eps$ of the best of a few close competitors. The measure is a count: the unique
human labels, pilot included, that the audit needs with and without the evaluator, after a judge-free annotation design
(skip known-zero differences, label identical outputs once) has been credited first. On WMT22 MQM (31 metric variants,
two open LLM judges), LMArena and retrieval, the design saves a median 4.6\% of uniform-sampling labels over 30 close MT
decisions (8--16\% in the pre-registered cells, 41--56\% in retrieval); the mean metric adds 2.3\% on top of it with a
refitted coefficient, and a 10\% increment is ruled out for 94\% of decision--evaluator pairs. Strong evaluators are
the exception: the best metric of each year (MetricX, XCOMET) adds 4--5\%, as much as the design itself in a third to
over half of the cells. The reason is the level of measurement: metrics with system-level correlation 0.93--0.99 correlate only
0.11--0.38 with the paired differences a close decision depends on, and the saving is about $\rho^2(1-P/J)$, diluted
by the pilot's share $P/J$ of the cost. Four auditor strategies fixed on WMT22 and applied unchanged to WMT23 (42
metrics) give the practical rule: deduplicate, add one strong evaluator fixed in advance and refit its coefficient
(3\% cheaper than the design alone, in all 26 informative held-out cells); choosing the evaluator from the pilot is no better (paired
difference $-0.1\%$, 95\% interval $[-0.7, +0.5]$ over 2,000 audits per cell), and with a frozen coefficient it costs 4\%.
The certificate holds its nominal error only from about 200 labels on; an auditor who waits for that keeps the
evaluator's saving only on the decisions that need more, 0.3--1.5\% on average. Three pre-registered locks; the failed
hypotheses are reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Human judgments remain the reference for comparing NLP systems, and they are expensive. Automatic evaluators promise to
stretch them: score everything automatically and spend human effort only on correcting the evaluator's bias, as in
prediction-powered inference \citep[PPI;][]{angelopoulos2023ppi,angelopoulos2023ppipp}. The saving is now measured
directly \citep{boyeau2024autoeval,chen2026noisy}, and \citet{gao2026ppsr} rank MT metrics by the annotation they save
(PPSR): under uniform labelling, with the coefficient fitted on the evaluation labels, the strongest WMT22 metrics save
about 9--15\% of the labels a paired comparison needs.

We do not revisit that number. We ask what is left of it for the decision an evaluation is run for, and how an auditor
should use it. A team has a short list of strong systems that are close in quality and wants to certify from a human
audit that the one it picks is within $\eps$ of the best. Three things separate this from the asymptotic picture.
The decision is \emph{local}: the systems that matter are the top few, not the field over which system-level
correlation is computed. The audit need not sample \emph{uniformly}: items whose paired difference is known to be zero
(identical outputs) need no label, and such labels are saved without any evaluator, which should be credited only with
what it adds on top. And the coefficient that corrects the evaluator must be \emph{estimated} from the audit's own
labels, either frozen after a small pilot or refitted as labels arrive.

Our method is correspondingly plain. We simulate the audit on data whose human labels exist, count the unique human labels
it needs to certify the decision, and define an evaluator's human-evidence saving (HES) as the share of those labels it
removes on top of the judge-free design (\S\ref{sec:setup}). A concrete case fixes the bookkeeping. Two translation
systems are to be compared on 1{,}000 source segments. On the segments where both produce the same string the quality
difference is zero and nothing need be rated; on the rest, a pilot of 50 segments is rated for both systems, the
candidate is the pilot leader, and a pre-committed number of further segments is rated; the decision is certified if
a one-sided bound on the competitor's lead is then below $\eps$. We report the budget at which half of the simulated
audits certify, not a sequential rule that stops when the bound first crosses $\eps$. An evaluator enters as a control variate: its score differences predict the human ones, so each rated
segment is worth more. Its value is the labels it removes from this audit, \emph{after} the identical segments have
been skipped and \emph{including} the pilot, which it cannot touch. The pilot matters: if the audit needs 250 labels
and the pilot is 100 of them, an evaluator that cuts the remaining 150 by 10\% saves 6\% of the total (hypothetical
numbers). Three findings follow (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The design comes first; the average evaluator adds little, the strongest as much again.} Over 30
two-system WMT22 decisions (2,000 simulated audits per cell, 31 metric variants) the judge-free design saves a median
4.6\% of uniform-sampling labels (8--16\% in the four pre-registered cells, 41--56\% in retrieval); on the same
design the mean metric adds 2.3\% with a refitted coefficient and 0.2\% with a frozen one, and a one-sided bound rules
out a 10\% increment for 94\% of decision--evaluator pairs. The strongest metric of each year (MetricX-XXL on WMT22,
XCOMET-XXL on WMT23) adds 4--5\%, more than the design itself in a third to over half of the cells.
\item \textbf{The reason is the level of measurement.} Metrics with system-level Pearson 0.93--0.99 correlate only
0.11--0.38 with the paired differences a close decision depends on, and the realised saving is about
$\rho^2(1-P/J)$: the squared decision-level correlation, diluted by the pilot's share of the cost (\S\ref{sec:why}).
\item \textbf{A pre-fixed strong evaluator is as good as choosing one from the pilot.} Four strategies fixed on
WMT22 and applied unchanged to WMT23 (42 metrics) give the same answer in both years: dedup plus one strong evaluator
fixed in advance, with a refitted coefficient, costs 3\% less than the design alone (in every WMT23 cell);
choosing the evaluator from the pilot instead changes the cost by $-0.1\%$ [$-0.7$, $+0.5$] in a paired comparison
at 2,000 audits per cell, and costs 4\% more when the coefficient is frozen. Under boundary stress the certificate
is at its nominal error only from about 200 labels on, for the human-only design as much as for the evaluators; a
protocol that waits for that range keeps 0.3--1.5\% of the evaluator's saving, because most of the 30 decisions
certify earlier (\S\ref{sec:heldout}).
\end{enumerate}
PPSR answers which evaluator saves most; we ask what the saving is worth once the decision is local, the design is
credited first and the coefficient is fitted on the audit. We propose no estimator: the corrected estimator is PPI++
with a control variate, the allocation is active inference \citep{zrnic2024active} for the decision's functional. Three
pre-registered locks fixed the hypotheses before evaluator outputs were joined with human labels; three of ten failed
and are reported (App.~\ref{app:prereg}).

\section{The decision and its cost}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Decision.} A menu $\mathcal M$ of systems is evaluated on a fixed set of $N$ items with human utilities
$u_m(i)$ and means $\mu_m$. An audit labels a pilot of $P$ items fully, picks the candidate $\hat m$ with the best pilot
mean, and \emph{certifies} it if for every competitor $j$ a one-sided upper bound on $\mu_j-\mu_{\hat m}$ is at most
$\eps$ (normal bound, Bonferroni over the $|\mathcal M|-1$ competitors, $\alpha=0.10$). A certificate is wrong if
$\max_j\mu_j-\mu_{\hat m}>\eps$. Menus are deliberately hard, built from the full human labels (the top-4 systems by
MQM, every pair among the top 6, the closest Arena pairs); their construction is not part of the audit.

\paragraph{Estimator.} After the pilot, items are drawn by Poisson sampling with probabilities $\pi_i$. For
$D_j=u_j-u_{\hat m}$ and an evaluator's predicted difference $\hat D_j$,
\begin{equation}
\hat\Delta_j=\tfrac1N\Big[\sum_{\text{pilot}}D_j+\sum_{\text{rest}}\lambda_j\hat D_j+\sum_{i\in S}\tfrac{D_j(i)-\lambda_j\hat D_j(i)}{\pi_i}\Big],\label{eq:est}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_j=\max(0,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(D_j,\hat D_j)/\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat D_j))$ the PPI++ coefficient,
either fitted on the pilot and frozen or refitted on every label as the audit proceeds; $\lambda=0$ is the human-only
estimator; where two outputs coincide, $\hat D_j(i)$ is set to 0 with $D_j(i)=0$ (the audit code does this
explicitly; 13--14 of the WMT22 and 26--27 of the WMT23 metrics score identical strings slightly differently, by at
most $2\times10^{-4}$ in the mean difference otherwise). With a frozen coefficient, (\ref{eq:est}) is a
Horvitz--Thompson estimator \citep{horvitz1952} and, given the pilot, unbiased for the finite-population mean for any
evaluator; its variance is estimated by $\sum_S(1-\pi_i)R_i^2/(\pi_i^2N^2)$, $R=D-\lambda\hat D$. With the
coefficient refitted on the sampled labels themselves, $\lambda$ depends on the sample, exact unbiasedness no longer
holds and the variance estimate ignores the coefficient's error, the plug-in bias \citet{chaganty2018price} note; we
therefore report the coverage of the refitted bound by simulation (\S\ref{sec:heldout}, App.~\ref{app:robust}).

\paragraph{Designs.} \emph{Uniform}: constant $\pi$, every menu output of a sampled item labelled, identical strings
included, as in WMT22's own MQM campaign (42\% of en$\to$de and 18\% of zh$\to$en rated outputs share their string with
another system's). \emph{Dedup}: uniform over the items where some competitor's output differs from the candidate's,
identical strings labelled once, and $D_j=0$ used exactly where outputs coincide. A \emph{weighted} variant that also
samples items in proportion to a label-free proxy of $|D|$ is sometimes cheaper and is reported in App.~\ref{app:sens};
the cheapest of these human-only designs in a cell is its \emph{judge-free baseline}, a benchmark chosen from realised
costs. An evaluator enters only as the control variate of (\ref{eq:est}) on top of a design.

\paragraph{Cost and HES.} $\Jt$ counts unique human labels, pilot included, at which a pre-committed budget certifies
in half of the simulated audits (300 or 2,000; linear interpolation over a geometric grid of 14 budgets). The human-evidence
saving of an evaluator is $\HES=1-\Jt(\text{design}+\text{evaluator})/\Jt(\text{design})$. The 50-segment MT pilot
costs 162 (en$\to$de) and 182 (zh$\to$en) labels, 42--68\% of $\Jt$ on en$\to$de; an evaluator can act only on the
rest, the factor $1-P/J$ of \S\ref{sec:why}. Intervals are paired bootstraps over the simulated audits of a fixed test
set, with one resampling of audit indices shared by every cell of a comparison (the cells share the simulator's random
streams): Monte-Carlo, not data-sampling, uncertainty.

\paragraph{Data and evaluators.} \textbf{MT:} WMT22 MQM \citep{freitag2021mqm,freitag2022wmt22metrics} en$\to$de
(1{,}315 segments) and zh$\to$en (1{,}875), Google weights, $u=-\min(\mathrm{MQM},25)/25$, identical strings of a
segment sharing the mean of their ratings; the 31 WMT22 metric variants available at segment level in mt-metrics-eval
(reference-based against refA, including COMET-22 \citep{rei2022comet}, and reference-free QE variants), plus our own
COMET-22 run and Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3} and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral} with the GEMBA-DA
prompt \citep{kocmi2023gemba}; pilot 50, $\eps\in\{0.01,0.02\}$. The held-out year is WMT23 MQM en$\to$de (460
paragraph-level segments, 11 systems) and zh$\to$en (1{,}177 segments, 14 systems; \citealp{freitag2023wmt23}) with
its 42 submitted metric variants, among them XCOMET \citep{guerreiro2024xcomet}, MetricX-23 \citep{juraska2023metricx23}
and the GPT-4-based GEMBA-MQM \citep{kocmi2023gembamqm} (App.~\ref{app:heldout}). \textbf{Chat:} the six English LMArena pairs \citep{chiang2024arena} with the smallest
win-rate gaps among those with $\ge300$ battles (gaps 0.011--0.084); Qwen3-8B and Mistral-7B as pairwise judges, both
presentation orders averaged, and ``the longer response wins''; pilot 50, $\eps\in\{0.05,0.1\}$. \textbf{Retrieval:} a
re-analysis of fixed-budget audits from an earlier study \citep{anonymous2026retrieval}: three cutoff policies compared
by precision at their cutoffs on four fully judged collections (ANTIQUE, CAsT 2019, DBpedia-Entity, TREC DL 21--23;
\citealp{hashemi2020antique,dalton2020cast,hasibi2017dbpedia,craswell2021dl21,craswell2022dl22,craswell2023dl23}) with
Qwen3-8B (UMBRELA prompt; \citealp{upadhyay2024umbrela}) and Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B \citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding};
pilot 20 queries.

\subsection{The design takes most of the saving}\label{sec:design}

Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} splits the saving over uniform sampling into the part the judge-free design obtains and the
part an evaluator adds on the same design, in the same audits: with $s_D$ the design's share and $h$ the evaluator's
HES over it, the total is $s_{\text{total}}=s_D+(1-s_D)h$, so the two are never added directly. In MT, identical
outputs (per pair of menu systems, 14--24\% of en$\to$de and 5--16\% of zh$\to$en segments) need no label and many
paired MQM differences are exactly zero (53--59\% and 40--48\%); skipping them saves a median 5.2\% (WMT22) and 3.8\%
(WMT23) of uniform-sampling labels over the two-system decisions, and 8--16\% in the four pre-registered top-4 cells
(App.~\ref{app:locked}, Table~\ref{tab:main}). In retrieval, where whole documents fall outside every cutoff, the
design saves 41--56\% and the Qwen3-8B judge adds 1--9 points.

\paragraph{The average evaluator adds little.} On the dedup design the mean of the submitted metrics adds a median
1.8\% (WMT22) and 2.5\% (WMT23) with a refitted coefficient, and nothing ($0.2\%$, $0.3\%$) with a coefficient
frozen after the pilot; on uniform sampling the same evaluators would add 2.1\% and 2.9\%, so the design takes
little of what they have to give, and in 69--79\% of cells it saves more labels than the mean evaluator adds. The
pre-fixed COMET-22 adds 2.9\% and 3.1\%.

\paragraph{Strong evaluators are the exception.} The strongest metric of each year, MetricX-XXL on WMT22 and
XCOMET-XXL on WMT23, adds a median 3.7\% and 4.7\% refitted, and the post-hoc best 6.8\% and 6.2\%. In labels, the
design saves more than XCOMET-XXL adds in only 11 of the 26 WMT23 cells (22 of 33 for MetricX-XXL on WMT22), and
more than the ten evaluators with the highest pilot $\rho$ in 44\% (WMT23) and 67\% (WMT22) of decision--evaluator
pairs. The statement ``the design saves more than the evaluator'' therefore holds for the typical evaluator and for
every evaluator with a frozen coefficient, not for the strongest with a refitted one; what the strongest cannot do is
to be identified in advance, which is the question of \S\ref{sec:heldout}. On the six close chat pairs the open
7--8B judges reach decision-level $\rho$ of $-0.01$ to 0.29 and save at least 5\% in two pairs (H6 required three;
App.~\ref{app:arena}).

\subsection{Thirty decisions: small, real, bounded}\label{sec:decisions}
Four cells are few. We therefore repeated the comparison on every two-system decision among the top six WMT22 systems of
each language pair: 30 decisions, two $\eps$ each, the 31 metric variants, 2,000 simulated audits per cell (the median
95\% interval of one evaluator's HES is then $\pm2.3$ points; the 300-audit runs with all 34 evaluators give the same
medians, App.~\ref{app:sens}). In 36 of the 60 cells at least 30\% of the uniform-sampling cost falls after the pilot;
in the others the pilot nearly settles the decision and nothing can save much. Over these 36 cells
(Figure~\ref{fig:decisions}) the judge-free design saves a median 4.6\%; the mean evaluator adds 0.2\% with the frozen
coefficient and 2.3\% refitted, the ten strongest 3.9\%, and the design saves more than the mean evaluator in 100\% and
89\% of cells. The post-hoc best (median 6.0\% refitted) leads the mean by more than selection noise explains (3.3 vs.\
1.8 points; $p<0.05$ in 72\% of cells), so evaluators do differ; the design still beats it in 39\% of cells. The
one-sided 95\% upper bound of a single evaluator's refitted HES is below 5\% in 64\% and below 10\% in 94\% of the 1,116
decision--evaluator pairs (29\% and 88\% for the ten strongest); 53\% of pairs are significantly above zero, with a
median 2.0\%. In labels: of a median decision's 256, the design saves 17, the mean evaluator 6, the post-hoc best 17.

\subsection{Held-out year: what an auditor should do}\label{sec:heldout}
Everything above chooses the cheapest design and the best evaluator after seeing the costs. An auditor cannot. We
therefore fixed four strategies on WMT22 and applied them, unchanged, to the WMT23 MQM sets (the same 15 two-system
menus per language pair, pilot 50, $\eps\in\{0.01,0.02\}$, 42 metric variants; pre-registered as lock v1.2 before any
WMT23 record was examined, App.~\ref{app:heldout}). S1: the dedup design alone (cheaper than weighted in 75--83\% of
WMT22 cells). S2: dedup plus COMET-22, the WMT22 metric with the highest decision-level $\rho$ among those submitted
under the same name in both years. S3: dedup plus the evaluator with the highest pilot $\rho$. S4: S3 unless that $\rho$
is below 0.2, the break-even of \S\ref{sec:why}; then S1. Each strategy picks one arm per audit from pilot information
only; its cost is $\Jt$ with the pilot included.

Table~\ref{tab:heldout} gives the outcome, and three paired comparisons answer three questions.

\emph{Is an evaluator worth adding?} S2 against S1: with the coefficient refitted, the pre-fixed COMET-22 costs
3.4\% less than the design alone on WMT23 (95\% Monte-Carlo interval [2.9, 3.9]; cheaper in all 26 informative cells,
21 of them with a per-cell interval above zero, and in 31 of the 60 cells overall, the other 29 being decisions settled
at the pilot where every strategy costs the same; WMT22: 4.4\% [2.1, 6.7], 85\% of cells). With the coefficient frozen
after the pilot it saves 0.4\% (WMT22: 2.2\%).

\emph{Is choosing the evaluator from the pilot worth more than fixing one in advance?} S3 against S2 on the same
audits, $G=1-\Jt(\mathrm{S3})/\Jt(\mathrm{S2})$: $-0.1\%$ on WMT23 (95\% Monte-Carlo interval $[-0.7, +0.5]$: the
mean over cells, with one resampling of the 2,000 audit indices applied to every cell at once, since the cells share
the simulator's random streams; the benchmark itself is fixed) and $-1.5\%$ on WMT22 ($[-4.4, +0.3]$, 300 audits).
Treating the decisions as clusters widens the intervals to $[-1.2, +1.1]$ and $[-8.9, +1.3]$ (App.~\ref{app:heldout}).
Neither year shows a gain from selection, and every interval lies below $+2$ points, the margin we set after the
300-audit results and before the 2,000-audit run (it is not part of lock v1.2): a one-sided check that selection does
not gain 2 points, not a two-sided equivalence test, and on WMT22 a loss of several points remains open. The 60 WMT23
cells give $-0.1\%$ $[-0.3, +0.2]$. Abstaining when the chosen evaluator's pilot $\rho$ is below 0.2 changes nothing (2--4\% of audits).
The post-hoc best is 7.0\% cheaper than S1 and is shown for reference only: it is chosen from the outcome. At 2,000
audits its lead over the \emph{mean} evaluator exceeds what selecting the largest of 42 equal evaluators produces in
81\% of cells (the test of \S\ref{sec:decisions}, not a test against S2; at 300 audits in none), so the evaluators do
differ; the pilot selection we tested (the highest $\rho$ on 50 segments) does not find the best one, and adds
nothing over the pre-fixed COMET-22.

\emph{Does the coefficient rule matter?} S3 frozen against S3 refitted: pilot selection costs 4.2\% \emph{more}
than the design with a frozen coefficient and 3.3\% less with a refitted one (WMT22: $+2.3\%$ and $-3.2\%$); half of
the frozen-coefficient selections are over 2\% dearer than the design, 8\% of the refitted ones. The price of a pilot
coefficient is the evaluator's whole value.

\emph{Validity.} On the 30 decisions themselves, whose true gaps are mostly well clear of $\eps$, every strategy
certifies at its $\Jt$ with a wrong-certificate rate of 0.003--0.006, equal to the human-only design's, and the chosen
arm's upper bound covers the true difference at 0.89--0.90 at that budget (nominal 0.90), at 0.90--0.91 averaged over
all budgets and at 0.86--0.89 at the worst budget of a cell, the same as the human-only design (0.86--0.89). This holds once known-zero differences are set to zero (\S\ref{sec:setup}); on records
that keep the small non-zero scores 26--27 of the WMT23 metrics give identical outputs, the refitted bound covered at
only 0.45--0.50 at the smallest budgets: the term $\lambda\sum_{\text{rest}}\hat D$ of (\ref{eq:est}) is then biased
on the known-zero items, and a coefficient fitted on few labels amplifies it (App.~\ref{app:heldout}).
Whether the certificate keeps its nominal error where it matters is a different question, answered under boundary
stress (the runner-up as candidate, $\eps$ at 0.9 times its regret, 300 audits), where every certificate is wrong. At
the smallest post-pilot budget (ten sampled items, about 20 labels on top of the pilot's 95) the type-I error is 0.18
for the human-only design, 0.17 for S2 and 0.12 for S3; it is not significantly above the nominal 0.10 from a total
of about 206 (en$\to$de) and 211 (zh$\to$en) labels on for every strategy, and 0.05 by about 450. The evaluator
strategies are below the human-only design on average (by 0.1--1.5 points) and above it at 15--34\% of the cell--budget
points, by more than 2 points at 1--3\%. The inflation is the normal bound on few items, and it applies to the
human-only baseline as much as to the evaluators, so it does not restore the certificate: the procedure is validated
only from about 200 labels on. Under a protocol that issues no certificate before that budget (55 and 61 post-pilot items, the same rule for every
strategy; App.~\ref{app:heldout}), the 21 informative cells that certify earlier tie, and over the 60 cells S2 saves
0.3\% [0.2, 0.4] over S1 with $G=-0.1\%$ $[-0.2, +0.0]$; the evaluator's saving survives only in the five cells that
need more than 200 labels (3.0--3.3\% in three of them). Tolerating a type-I error of 0.125 validates the certificate
from about 140--150 labels (23 and 29 items), where S2 saves 1.5\% [1.3, 1.7] over 56 informative cells and
$G=+0.0\%$ $[-0.2, +0.3]$. The 3\% of Table~\ref{tab:heldout} is therefore the saving of an auditor who takes the
certificate at face value at 140--200 labels, where its boundary type-I error is 0.11--0.12 rather than 0.10; the
ranking of the strategies is the same under every rule, and what shrinks is the room any evaluator has. The new judges raise decision-level $\rho$ from 0.28 to 0.36
(MetricX-23, XCOMET; GEMBA-MQM 0.22). The rule that survives both years is short: deduplicate, add one strong
evaluator fixed in advance, refit its coefficient, and do not spend the pilot on choosing the evaluator or on
committing to the coefficient.

\section{Why: the level of measurement and the cost of the pilot}\label{sec:why}
\paragraph{Evaluator quality collapses across levels.} Among the 11 WMT22 submissions the strong metrics correlate with
MQM at 0.93--0.99 at system level. Among the four closest systems the ranking is fragile: MetricX-XL-DA and
MetricX-XXL-DA reach system-level Pearson 0.994--0.995 on zh$\to$en and Kendall $\tau=0$ among the top four, and 10 of
the 31 zh$\to$en variants (system-level 0.83--0.96; BLEU, spBLEU, chrF, BERTScore, YiSi, SEScore, MEE) rank them in
exactly reversed order. The quantity that governs a control variate is the correlation $\rho$ of per-item paired
differences, and it is low everywhere: 0.30--0.38 for the best metrics on en$\to$de, 0.21--0.25 on zh$\to$en, 0.17--0.29
for COMET-22 and the Qwen3-8B judge, 0.04--0.09 for Mistral-7B (Figure~\ref{fig:levels} in App.~\ref{app:locked}).
Part of this is label noise: in the en$\to$de three-rater re-annotation two raters' paired differences correlate only
0.31--0.41, so even a perfect evaluator would reach $\rho\approx0.59$ against the single ratings an audit collects; the
best metrics attain one half to two thirds of that (App.~\ref{app:sens}). HES is ranked slightly better by $\rho$ than
by system-level Pearson (Spearman 0.39 vs.\ 0.36 on en$\to$de, 0.22 vs.\ 0.07 on zh$\to$en; H11), but neither predicts
it well, because every real evaluator sits where the saving is small.

\paragraph{The saving is about $\rho^2(1-P/J)$.} A control variate with correlation $\rho$ reduces the residual variance
by the factor $1-\rho^2$ \citep{angelopoulos2023ppipp}; with $\lambda$ estimated on a pilot of $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$
informative items and frozen, the factor becomes $(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ \citep{cochran1977}; and only the
post-pilot share $1-P/J_0$ of the cost can be saved. To first order,
\begin{equation}
\HES\approx\Big[\rho^2-\frac{1-\rho^2}{P_{\mathrm{eff}}}\Big]\Big(1-\frac{P}{J_0}\Big),\qquad J_0=P+L_0,\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
an interpretive approximation (homoskedastic linear projection, normal fixed-budget approximation; derivation and the
design-optimal coefficient in App.~\ref{app:law}), not a bound. It says three things that the data confirm. A frozen
coefficient costs labels when $\rho^2<1/(P_{\mathrm{eff}}+1)$, about $\rho<0.2$ for the 50-item MT pilot where half the
paired differences are zero; this is why pilot selection loses in \S\ref{sec:heldout} and why the frozen-coefficient
``tax'' is 4.3 [3.5, 5.2] (en$\to$de) and 2.5 [2.0, 3.1] (zh$\to$en) points over the 31 metrics (H9), removed by
refitting on all labels (coverage of the upper bound 0.90 at nominal 0.90; App.~\ref{app:tax}, \ref{app:robust}). Refitted savings follow
the tax-free approximation $\rho^2(1-P/J)$ (median deviation $-0.4$ points over 1{,}158 decision--evaluator points),
and it is below 5\% for 94\% of them, because $\rho\le0.43$ and the pilot is a median 39\% of the cost. Under controlled variation
(semi-synthetic evaluators with $\rho$ set to 0.1--0.9, 126 cells) predicted and realised HES correlate 0.97, with 10\%
realised at $\rho=0.5$ and 31\% at $\rho=0.8$ (Figure~\ref{fig:dial}, App.~\ref{app:dial}). The pilot size does not change the picture:
pilots of 25 and 10 segments give the mean evaluator 0.7\% and 0.3\% refitted and $-2.7\%$ and $-7.7\%$ frozen,
against design savings of 4.3--4.4\% (App.~\ref{app:sens}).

\paragraph{When the answer changes.} The three factors of (\ref{eq:law}) say where these results do and do not
transfer. When decision-level $\rho$ is low, as for every WMT22 metric and most WMT23 ones, the increment is a few
percent whatever the design; the exception in \S\ref{sec:design} is exactly the evaluators with $\rho$ above 0.3.
When the pilot is a large share of the cost, as on the paragraph-level WMT23 en$\to$de set (53\%) or whenever the
decision is easy, little remains for any evaluator to save, which is why we report only cells with at least 30\% of
the cost after the pilot. When a strong evaluator is used with a refitted coefficient, the increment can match the
design's own saving; with a frozen coefficient it cannot, because the tax $(1-\rho^2)/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ consumes it.
What we count is human labels: the evaluator's own cost, annotation time per label and the price of organising
deduplication are outside the measure. A label-cost model would re-weight the bars of Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}; we
did not measure those costs, so the ordering by total cost is not established here.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{The unit of evaluation.} System-level correlation is driven by outliers \citep{mathur2020tangled}, close
systems are better compared by pairwise accuracy \citep{kocmi2021ship,deutsch2023ties}, correlations on close pairs are
much lower \citep{deutsch2022reexamining}, and such comparisons are often underpowered \citep{card2020little}. JuStRank
\citep{gera2025justrank} and \citet{gao2025reeval} show that instance-level accuracy does not determine system-ranking
quality and that ranking degrades for close systems; \citet{zhang2026utility} separate judgment quality from downstream
utility in training and inference. We take the unit one step further, to the human labels a close decision requires.
\paragraph{Saving human labels with an evaluator.} \citet{chaganty2018price} asked our question first: using automatic
metrics as control variates on human judgments of summarisation and question answering, they obtained savings of
7--13\%, showed that a metric's high system-level correlation does not carry over to the instance level where the
control variate acts, that human rating noise caps the attainable saving, and that a coefficient estimated on the same
data is slightly biased. PPI and PPI++ \citep{angelopoulos2023ppi,angelopoulos2023ppipp} turned this into a general
estimator; AutoEval \citep{boyeau2024autoeval}, prediction-powered ranking \citep{chatzi2024ppr}, noisy-judge inference
\citep{chen2026noisy}, PRECISE \citep{divekar2026precise} and \citet{gligoric2024unconfident} apply it to LLM
evaluation, and \citet{dorner2025limits} bound the saving of an evaluator no better than the evaluated model. Closest
to us, \citet{gao2026ppsr} rank WMT metrics by the annotation they save under prediction-powered evaluation (PPSR, the
mean squared correlation of paired differences), test system pairs, and verify the saving by resampling at 80\% power
under uniform labelling with the coefficient fitted on the evaluation labels. Their saving and ours are not directly
comparable (power 80\% vs.\ our $\Jt$; uniform vs.\ dedup; all pairs vs.\ the closest), which is why
Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} measures the evaluator on both designs in the same audits.
\paragraph{Choosing what humans label.} Which items humans label is studied for model comparison
\citep{sawade2012active}, active testing \citep{kossen2021active} and IR test collections
\citep{sakai2016topic,li2017active,oosterhuis2024reliable}; within prediction-powered inference, active inference
\citep{zrnic2024active} allocates labels by the evaluator's uncertainty, stratified PPI \citep{fisch2024stratified}
fits the coefficient per stratum, and cross-prediction \citep{zrnic2024cross} splits the labels between fitting the
predictor and inference (our cross-fitted coefficient borrows the split, not the method). These works show that the
design and the evaluator both set the cost; the evaluator-driven designs are outside what we measure.
\paragraph{What this paper adds.} The increment of an evaluator used as a control variate once a judge-free design
has been credited and the pilot paid, measured as the labels a close decision needs; the finding that this increment is
small for the typical evaluator and comparable to the design's own saving for the strongest; and a test, with rules
fixed on one year and applied to the next, of whether an auditor can obtain it without choosing the evaluator after
the fact.

\section{Conclusion: three numbers to check before an audit}
For a close system decision, an evaluator's value is set by the correlation of the differences the decision depends on,
diluted by the pilot's fixed cost and by what a judge-free design already obtains; WMT22 and WMT23 metrics then save a
few percent of human labels on average, the strongest somewhat more, and the typical one less than organising the
annotation itself. A team can check three numbers before paying for an evaluator: (i) its decision-level $\rho$ on the
systems at stake, from a prior campaign or the pilot, not its system-level agreement; (ii) the pilot's share of the
cost, $P/J$, which a human-only pilot prediction supplies; (iii) what the judge-free design already saves: its duplicate and known-zero structure is known in advance, the
$\Jt$ it saves has to be estimated, as here by simulation. Under (\ref{eq:law}) the increment is about $\rho^2(1-P/J)$: for the
WMT metrics ($\rho\le0.43$, $P/J\approx0.4$) up to about 11\%, typically 2--5\%, against design savings of 5--16\%. The strategy these numbers imply,
deduplicate, add one strong evaluator fixed in advance, refit its coefficient, saved 3\% over the design on a held-out
year when the certificate is taken at face value, and 0.3--1.5\% under a protocol that certifies only in the budget
range where the bound's error is validated; choosing the evaluator from the pilot instead was not measurably better,
and freezing the coefficient cost the evaluator's whole value.

\section*{Limitations}
The menus are built from full human labels to be deliberately hard; easier decisions leave less to save for any method.
The certificate uses a normal bound whose calibration we checked by simulation, not proved; under boundary stress its
type-I error is above nominal below about 200 labels, for every strategy, and the savings reported at face value
include decisions that certify in that range (\S\ref{sec:heldout}). MT savings are computed on
the MQM-rated subsets of WMT22 (two language pairs), chat on six pairs from a 2023 Arena release, retrieval on
re-analysed audits of an earlier study with two judges. The cost approximation is first-order; it describes
controlled variation of $\rho$ and the scale of real savings, but does not predict them cell by cell. The LLM judges we ran are open 7--8B models; WMT23 contributes the submitted scores of the GPT-4-based GEMBA-MQM
($\rho$ 0.22), but we ran no new inference with frontier models, whose decision-level $\rho$ may be higher. The evaluator enters only as a control
variate on the audit; its other uses (choosing the candidate, building the menu, stratifying the sample) are outside
the decision value we measure. HES depends on the pilot size, and larger pilots
lower the estimation tax while raising the fixed cost (\S\ref{sec:why}). The results should not be read as showing
that automatic evaluators are useless: they concern close decisions, small human pilots and evaluators with
decision-level $\rho\le0.43$; by the cost law, an evaluator with $\rho\approx0.8$ would save about a third of the labels with our pilots, and more
with a smaller pilot share, but against single WMT22 ratings label noise caps $\rho$ near 0.6 (App.~\ref{app:sens}), so such
savings also require more reliable human labels. Uncertainty is Monte-Carlo over simulated audits of one fixed test set per language pair;
items and raters are not resampled. We report $\Jt$; at 80\% power savings differ and are often censored by the budget
grid (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). The estimand treats identical strings as one quality; sharing one random rating per string
instead leaves the design savings essentially unchanged (App.~\ref{app:sens}), but under an as-rated estimand, in which identical
strings may differ in quality, their rating noise would be variance that deduplication does not remove. The retrieval evidence rests on a companion study
under review.

\section{Derivation of the cost approximation}\label{app:law}
Write $D$ for the paired difference of an informative item, $\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(D)$, and $\hat D$ for the evaluator's
prediction with $\mathrm{Corr}(D,\hat D)=\rho$. For a fixed coefficient $\lambda$ the residual $R=D-\lambda\hat D$ has
variance $\sigma^2-2\lambda\rho\sigma\hat\sigma+\lambda^2\hat\sigma^2$, minimised at $\lambda^\star=\rho\sigma/\hat\sigma$ to
$\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)$ \citep{angelopoulos2023ppipp}. With $\hat\lambda$ the least-squares coefficient from a
pilot of effective informative size $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$, independent of the post-pilot sample, and a homoskedastic residual ($\mathrm{E}[R^2\mid\hat D]=\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)$), $\mathrm{E}[(\hat\lambda-\lambda^\star)^2]\approx
\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)/(P_{\mathrm{eff}}\hat\sigma^2)$, so the expected residual variance is
$\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ \citep[\S7]{cochran1977}. Under the normal fixed-budget approximation the
post-pilot labels needed to bring $z\sqrt{v/L}$ below the slack scale with the variance factor, so
$L\approx L_0(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ and $1-L/L_0\approx\rho^2-(1-\rho^2)/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ to first order in
$1/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$. With the pilot cost $P$ common to both arms, $\HES=1-(P+L)/(P+L_0)=(1-L/L_0)(1-P/J_0)$. Clipping
$\lambda$ at zero bounds the loss of an evaluator with $\rho\le0$ by the variance of $\hat\lambda$ only; zero-difference
items reduce $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ below $P$, which is why MT, with about half its paired differences zero, pays more tax.
In general, $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\lambda)\approx\mathrm{E}[(\hat D-\mathrm{E}\hat D)^2R^2]/(P_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat D)^2)$ (sandwich form), which reduces to the expression above under homoskedasticity; heavy-tailed or heteroskedastic residuals, as with sparse MQM differences, raise the tax.
The coefficient that minimises the variance of (\ref{eq:est}) under a given design, $\sum_i(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i\,(D_i-\lambda\hat D_i)^2$, is a
least-squares fit with weights $(1-\pi_i)/\pi_i$ and equals the population coefficient only under uniform sampling; on the
weighted design of the top-4 menus (31 metrics, three semi-synthetic evaluators, 20 pilots) the population coefficient's variance
exceeds it by a median 0.0\%, a 90th percentile of 0.2\% and at most 3.7\%, so $\rho^2$ is used as an interpretive approximation, not a bound.

\section{The estimation tax on real evaluators}\label{app:tax}
\paragraph{The estimation tax on real metrics.} Replacing the pilot-fitted coefficient by the population-optimal one
isolates the tax. Over the 31 metric variants it is 4.3 [3.5, 5.2] points on en$\to$de and 2.5 [2.0, 3.1] on zh$\to$en
(metric-level bootstrap; H9). For the five metrics with the largest $\rho^2$ (0.11--0.15 on en$\to$de, as $(\bar\rho)^2$
or as PPSR's mean $\rho^2$, within the 0.09--0.15 PPSR reports), the realised saving over uniform sampling is between
$-4\%$ and $+6\%$ (en$\to$de) and $-6\%$ and $+1\%$ (zh$\to$en), below the correlation-based saving in every cell (H10,
under both definitions). With the oracle coefficient the same en$\to$de metrics reach 6--12\% on the decision-aware
design: the asymptotic saving is not wrong, it is what an auditor who already knew $\lambda$ would obtain.

\paragraph{Is the tax an artefact of freezing $\lambda$?} Mostly. Standard PPI++ fits $\lambda$ on all labelled
items. We re-ran the eight strongest metric variants per language pair, COMET-22 and the two GEMBA judges with the
coefficient refitted on the pilot and every post-pilot label, and cross-fitted over two folds (App.~\ref{app:robust}). Mean HES on the weighted design rises from $+0.4\%$ (pilot coefficient) to
$+6.8\%$ with refitting and $+1.6\%$ with cross-fitting on en$\to$de, against $+6.7\%$ for the oracle coefficient; on
zh$\to$en from $-0.4\%$ to $+2.0\%$ and $+1.1\%$, against $+1.3\%$; on the six chat pairs from $+1.1\%$ to $+2.3\%$ and $+1.5\%$, against $+2.1\%$. Its variance estimate ignores the coefficient's estimation
error, but over the 30 decisions and the six chat pairs the refitted upper bound covered the true difference at 0.90
(nominal 0.90), also at the budget nearest each design's $\Jt$, where it certifies (refit 0.902, cross-fit 0.903,
pilot 0.903, human-only 0.904), and under boundary stress its largest type-I error was 0.05. Cross-fitting recovers
less because each fold's coefficient rests on half of the labels; a practitioner who needs no budget plan can refit,
one who wants the variance estimate to account for the coefficient should cross-fit or freeze (the bound itself is
calibrated by simulation, not proved). Refitting can exceed the oracle, which is the population
least-squares coefficient, optimal for uniform sampling only; on the weighted design the coefficient that minimises the
realised Poisson-design variance differs from it by a median 0.0\% and at most 3.7\% of that variance (App.~\ref{app:law}), so the two are interchangeable here. Across the 30 decisions (Figure~\ref{fig:decisions}b) refitted HES
follows the tax-free approximation $\rho^2(1-P/J)$ (median deviation $-0.4$ points, correlation 0.42 over 1{,}158
decision--evaluator points), whereas with the pilot coefficient it falls a median 2.3 points below it; 94\% of the
approximate values are below 5\%, because decision-level $\rho$ is at most 0.43 and the pilot is a median 39\% of the cost (range 15--66\%). The tax is therefore the price of committing to the coefficient before the audit (to plan the budget, say), not a
property of the evaluator; refitting remains exploratory (the same labels set the coefficient and the inference). Even refitted, in the four locked cells the mean of the eleven (6.9, 6.7, 4.8, $-0.3\%$) stays below the design
(15.9, 9.0, 8.4, 8.5\%).

\paragraph{Pilot size trades tax for fixed cost.} Equation~(\ref{eq:law}) predicts that a larger pilot lowers the tax
$(1-\rho^2)/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ but raises the fixed cost $P$. Both effects appear. Doubling the MT pilot to 100 segments
shrinks the losses of the weak LLM judges (Mistral-7B, en$\to$de, $\eps=0.01$: $-7.1\%\to-0.9\%$) and halves the saving of a semi-synthetic $\rho=0.9$ evaluator (32\%$\to$15\%) because the
pilot becomes most of the cost. A smaller pilot does not rescue the evaluators either: with pilots of 25 and 10 segments
on the 30 two-system decisions (49 and 60 informative cells, now including en$\to$de; pilot share 27\% and 13\% of the
cost; the 31 metric variants), the design saves a median 4.3\% and 4.4\%, the mean evaluator adds 0.7\% and 0.3\%
refitted ($-2.7\%$ and $-7.7\%$ with the pilot coefficient, whose tax grows as $1/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$), and the design
saves more in 86\% and 90\% of cells (App.~\ref{app:sens}).

The finite-pilot statement behind (\ref{eq:law}):
\begin{proposition}[Finite-pilot cost approximation]\label{prop:law}
Let a design need $L_0$ post-pilot labels for a fixed decision without an evaluator, and let the evaluator's paired
differences $\hat D$ have correlation $\rho$ with the human ones $D$. Assume that, over informative items (those with a
non-zero difference), $D$ is a linear projection on $\hat D$ with a residual whose variance does not depend on $\hat D$
(homoskedastic; joint normality is a sufficient case), and that $\lambda$ is fitted by least squares on
a pilot independent of the post-pilot sample, with effective informative size $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (the number of pilot
items that contribute variation to the paired regression, approximated by those with a non-zero paired difference; $\lambda_j$ and $P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ are per competitor $j$). Then, to first order in
$1/P_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and under the normal fixed-budget approximation, the variance of the corrected estimator is
$(1-\rho^2)(1+1/P_{\mathrm{eff}})$ times that of the uncorrected one and
\begin{equation}
\HES\approx\Big[\rho^2-\frac{1-\rho^2}{P_{\mathrm{eff}}}\Big]\Big(1-\frac{P}{J_0}\Big),\qquad J_0=P+L_0 .\label{eq:law-prop}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Informativeness threshold.} The post-pilot share $1-P/J$ of the 60 MT cells takes no value between 0.21 and
0.47, so every threshold in that range selects the same 33 cells. With a threshold of 0.2 (34 cells), 0.5 (30) or 0.6

1.7\%, and the design beats the mean evaluator in 94/82/91, 93/83/90 and 91/78/87\% of cells (pilot/refit/cross-fit).

\paragraph{Evaluator set.} Restricting the mean to the ten evaluators per language pair with the highest system-level
Pearson, chosen without reference to any decision, raises the mean evaluator's median HES on the 33 cells to 0.6, 2.8 and
1.8\% (pilot, refit, cross-fit); the design still saves more in 91, 76 and 79\% of cells.

\paragraph{One rating per identical string.} WMT22 rated identical strings separately, and our estimand averages their
ratings. To check that the deduplication saving does not depend on this averaging, each group of identical strings
instead shares one of its ratings, drawn at random (\texttt{--ident pick}). In the four locked cells the best fixed design
then saves 17.0, 8.5, 9.2 and 12.2\% (means over three draws of the shared rating; ranges 15.5--18.4, 7.9--8.8, 7.9--10.0
and 11.0--13.9) instead of 15.9, 9.0, 8.4 and 8.5\%. Over the 30 decisions (again 33 informative cells) the design's median saving is 5.2\% instead of 5.5\%, the mean
evaluator's median HES $-0.6$, 1.4 and 0.1\% (pilot, refit, cross-fit) instead of $-0.1$, 1.6 and 0.4\%, and the design
beats it in 91, 85 and 91\% of the cells instead of 94, 82 and 91\%. The deduplication saving therefore does not rest on
the averaging.

\paragraph{Dissimilarity bins.} The weighted design bins the positive chrF dissimilarities into three groups (below the
median, third and fourth quartile; identical outputs have weight zero). With four quartile bins instead, the design's
$\Jt$ in the four locked cells changes by at most 0.8\% (weighted: 390 vs.\ 389, 238 vs.\ 238, 2{,}743 vs.\ 2{,}767 and
1{,}103 vs.\ 1{,}095 labels), the best fixed design's saving by at most 0.1 points, and the best metric's increment by
at most 1.2 points; over the 30 two-system decisions (the same 33 informative cells, 31 metric variants) the design's
median saving is 5.5\% under both binnings and the mean evaluator's HES $-0.5$ / 1.3 / 0.2\% (pilot / refit / cross-fit)
against $-0.1$ / 1.6 / 0.3\% with three bins (supplementary \texttt{results/VARIANTS.md}).

\paragraph{Monte-Carlo precision.} With 2,000 audits per cell (31 metric variants) the informative cells are 36
rather than 33 (en$\to$de decisions 13 and 24 at $\eps=0.01$ and zh$\to$en 05 at $\eps=0.02$ cross the 0.3 threshold,
with post-pilot shares 0.37--0.42); the design's median saving is 4.6\% (300 audits: 5.5\%), the mean evaluator's
0.2 / 2.3 / 0.9\% ($-0.1$ / 1.6 / 0.4\%), and for the ten strongest evaluators 1.4 / 3.9 / 2.2\% (0.6 / 2.8 / 1.8\%); the
design saves more in 100 / 89 / 100\% of cells (89 / 61 / 78\% against the ten strongest). The one-sided bounds of
\S\ref{sec:decisions} are in supplementary \texttt{results/EXCLUSION\_n2000.md}; the 300-audit runs remain the paper's
primary analysis because they include the three LLM judges.

\paragraph{Pilot size.} The 30 two-system decisions re-run with pilots of 25 and 10 segments (the 31 metric
variants; the same menus, $\eps$ and coefficient rules). Informative cells: 49 and 60 (pilot 50: 33); pilot share of the
cost, median: 27\% and 13\% (35\%); design saving, median: 4.3\% and 4.4\% (5.5\%); mean evaluator HES, median over
cells, pilot / refit / cross-fit: $-2.7$ / 0.7 / 0.0\% and $-7.7$ / 0.3 / 0.0\% (pilot 50: $-0.1$ / 1.6 / 0.4\%); for the
ten evaluators with the highest system-level correlation $-2.4$ / 2.2 / 0.0\% and $-6.8$ / 0.5 / 0.0\%; the design saves
more than the mean evaluator in 98 / 86 / 92\% and 100 / 90 / 98\% of cells. The frozen-coefficient tax grows as the
pilot shrinks, as (\ref{eq:law}) predicts, and refitting leaves the increment below 1\%.

\paragraph{Evaluators on the weighted vs.\ the dedup base.} Lock v1.1 applied every metric on the weighted design and
compared it with the cheapest judge-free design, which on zh$\to$en is dedup; Table~\ref{tab:main} now applies the metrics
on that same base. On zh$\to$en at $\eps=0.01$ the best metric adds 7.3\% [$-6$, 19] on dedup against $-0.9\%$ on
weighted; at $\eps=0.02$, 1.3\% against 0.8\%. The H8 verdict is unchanged either way, and the locked numbers are kept in
\texttt{results/MTME\_VERDICTS.md}.

\paragraph{Human noise ceiling.} WMT22 re-annotated en$\to$de with three raters per item. For each of the 15 pairs of the
top six systems, the paired differences of two distinct raters (drawn at random per item, 200 repetitions) correlate
0.31--0.41 (median 0.35; the same without identical outputs). Since the audits' labels are single ratings, an evaluator
that knew the true difference would reach at most $\rho\approx\sqrt{0.35}=0.59$ (0.55--0.64 per pair) against them, and
$\rho^2\approx0.35$ caps its tax-free saving at about a third of the post-pilot labels. The best en$\to$de metrics
($\rho$ 0.30--0.38) reach one half to two thirds of that ceiling, and their $\rho^2$ a quarter to two fifths of it, so
both label noise and the evaluators limit the saving.
